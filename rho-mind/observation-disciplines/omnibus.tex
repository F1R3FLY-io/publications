\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A logic, on the account developed here, is not a thing one chooses and then
applies to a computational system. It is a thing the system \emph{generates}.
Fix a term language and its rewrite rules, fix a container in which witnesses
are aggregated, and fix an algebra in which observations take their values, and
the connectives follow: the term constructors lift to structural predicates, the
contexts labelling rewrites lift to modalities, and the container's own
operations supply whatever propositional structure the logic is going to have.
Turning those dials does not produce variations on one logic. It produces a
range of genuinely different \emph{observation disciplines} over the same term
structure --- disciplines that differ in what they can distinguish, what they
cost to evaluate, and what they are able to learn. Classical linear logic,
intuitionistic logic, the logic of actionable coalitions, spatial-behavioural
logic, and the fuzzy and complex-valued logics of graded execution are exhibited
here as settings of the same generator rather than as separate inventions.

The second half of the thesis concerns placement. The same formula can be used
as a specification checked from outside, as a guard evaluated at the redex, or
as a hypothesis revised over time. It is the second placement --- the
\emph{where-clause} of a for-comprehension --- that turns a logic from a
description into an instrument, because it is there that the formula's value
resolves the system's own non-determinism. Allowing that value to be graded
rather than crisp is a small syntactic change with large consequences: it gives
a resource-bounded learner a coordinate in which to record disappointment, and
so a way to revise a belief without revising a formula.

This is a summary document. It assembles results established in a series of
research notes, restates them in a common vocabulary, and argues that they are
one result seen from several sides. Where something new is claimed, it is
flagged as such and usually left as a conjecture.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Reader's guide}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Reader's guide}

This document sprawls, deliberately and in a known tradition. It is meant to be
entered at several points.

\paragraph{Part 0} states the whole argument in about ten pages with no category
theory and no formal semantics. A reader who wants to know what is being claimed
and why it might matter should read Part 0 and stop; everything after it is
elaboration and evidence. Every subsequent part opens with a plain-language
paragraph, set off before any symbols appear, saying what that part establishes.

\paragraph{Parts I and II} are the generation story. Part I gives the generator:
what a logic is made of, which parameters it has, and in what sense the output is
not \emph{ad hoc}. Part II works the parameter that is least familiar --- the
container in which witnesses are collected --- and shows that the substructural
hierarchy, intuitionistic logic, classical linear logic and the logic of
decentralised consensus are four settings of it.

\paragraph{Parts III to V} are the instrument story: where a formula is placed
(Part III), what happens when its truth values are graded (Part IV), and what
resolving the resulting non-determinism costs (Part V).

\paragraph{Parts VI to VIII} are the evidence. Part VI walks six observation
disciplines with measured examples drawn from games, foraging, quantum circuits,
stampeding herds and honeybee swarms. Part VII is the learning argument proper
and the fractal it lives in. Part VIII inventories what is shown, what is not,
and what is open.

\paragraph{Sources.} Nothing here is offered as a replacement for the notes it
summarises. The technical arguments live in
\cite{Zeus,GradedWhere,ChoiceFormulae,QuorumNote,ScientistNote,GameNote,ComposeNote,EngineNote,ScopeNote,WeightedGSLT,GsltOmnibus,FuzzyRho,ChoiceScheduling,ConsensusTypes},
and each part names which of them it is compressing.

\paragraph{Two notational conventions, stated once.}
The reflective higher-order calculus is written \rhoc{} calculus, never with the
Greek letter that follows $\pi$. The name is an acronym and a pun at the same
time --- r.h.o.\ for \emph{reflective higher order}, and the transliteration of
the letter after $\pi$, marking the calculus as the successor of the
$\pi$-calculus --- and writing it as a Greek letter loses both. Quotation is
written $\quot{P}$ and dereference $\drop{x}$; the corner-quote notation of the
2005 papers is not used.
