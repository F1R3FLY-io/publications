\title{\bf \flangbf{} for the Working Software Developer\\[2mm]
\large From \texttt{SELECT-FROM-WHERE} to \texttt{SELECT-FROM-WHERE-DO},\\
\large and what happens when the \texttt{WHERE} stops answering yes or no}

\author{L.G. Meredith\\ \small F1R3FLY.io}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most treatments of a programming language begin with its data definition language
and arrive at control flow later. This one goes the other way round, because the
control flow language of \flang{} has a door every working developer has
already walked through: the query comprehension. \texttt{SELECT-FROM-WHERE} in SQL,
\texttt{from-where-select} in LINQ, \texttt{for-let-where-return} in XQuery --- one
shape, everywhere, for forty years. LINQ exists because that shape was worth
having inside a host language where a developer could finally say what to
\emph{do} with the result. \flang{} goes the rest of the way and builds the
language around the comprehension. Its receipt,
\texttt{for( ptrn <- chan where cond )P}, is \texttt{SELECT-FROM-WHERE-DO}; its
dual, \texttt{chan!(Q)}, makes the write side just as explicit as the read
side.

Making both sides explicit buys something a query language cannot have. A
communication is a meeting between a named reader and a named writer, and the
substitution performed by the communication rule is the \emph{witness} that the
meeting occurred --- so a transaction is a fact about the program text rather
than a service of the engine. That raises the question the second half of this
note answers: where do the pattern and the condition come from? They come from
\flang{}'s \texttt{Theory} declaration, a data definition language with four
rungs --- grammar, equations, rewrites, and parameters --- whose third rung
defines whole languages, whose data can wiggle, and whose fourth rung makes a
language definition a \emph{module}: a theory takes other theories as arguments,
renames what it inherits, and combines with its siblings over a shared ancestor
rather than beside one. That structure subsumes SingularityNET's MeTTa: MeTTa's
rules are the rewrites and its atom spaces are the channels, but MeTTa has no
equational layer and no notion of a witnessed transaction.

Two widenings follow. First, the condition language is not fixed: it is
\emph{generated} from the language definition, and the choice of container for
witnesses --- sets, quantales, join semi-lattices --- turns a dial between
Boolean, linear and coalition observation, giving a \texttt{where} clause
different powers of sight. Second, the condition need not be crisp. Graded
conditions take values in a resolution algebra, and at that point the same
program text stops being a state transition inside a node and becomes a
simulation of a population of programs --- stochastic over the non-negative
reals, quantum over the complex numbers. A developer moves between programming
in the small and programming in the large without changing languages, and
without maintaining a model separate from the system.

A single running example, a foraging learner drawn from the mortal scientist
programme, carries the exposition from the first \texttt{SELECT} to the last
simulation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}\small
\textbf{A note on the name.} This language has been carried in the research
notes under the code name \emph{MeTTaIL}, and before that as \emph{rholang
1.4}. Its name is \flang{}. The two glyphs in the middle of it are not
decoration: \texttt{|} is parallel composition and \texttt{@} is quotation, the
two operators everything else in the language is assembled from. Where earlier
notes say MeTTaIL, read \flang{}. \flang{} inherits a good part of its control
flow sublanguage from \emph{rholang}, and this note says so where the debt is
worth naming; but the inheritance is not an equivalence, and
\S\ref{sec:graded} is the reason. A \texttt{where} clause that answers with a
value rather than a verdict is not a convenience on top of rholang. It changes
what a program means.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
