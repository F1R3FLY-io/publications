\large Group-valued data, a chaos constant, and an equivariant COMM\\
for the reflective higher-order calculus}
\author{L.\,G.\ Meredith\\ \small F1R3FLY.io}
\date{October 18, 2026 \quad\textendash\quad Draft 3}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We take the pattern-matching variant of the rho calculus parametric in a type
of data, and specialise the data to a group $G$. The group acts on processes
freely: an element $g$ multiplies the datum of every name, descending
recursively through quotation, and the multiplication is deferred at variables
until they are instantiated. To the calculus we add an annihilating constant
$\chaos$, read as chaos or divergence, with $\chaos \mid P \scong \chaos$,
and an idempotent summation. The communication rule is generalised so that
a receive and a send on two orientations of the same channel produce a sum of
the continuation transported by the relative orientation and by its inverse.
We show that the action is an automorphism of the calculus, that chaos is
forced to have every name free, and that reduction is $G$-equivariant for an
arbitrary group exactly when the two coefficients are chosen as
$\nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1}$ and $\nu(u)\nu(x)^{-1}$. Because the action is deferred,
substitution and matching commute with it outright, and the action is free on
open terms as well as closed ones. It then yields an orbit-counting theorem:
the symmetry group of a process with finitely many free atoms acts freely on
them, so its order divides their number. For
rotation groups this is a crystallographic restriction on processes. Three
applications indicate the utility of the structure: symmetry reduction for
state-space exploration, where the symmetry is read off the term rather than
declared; symmetric rings, including the dining philosophers, as orbit
products; and \emph{twisted roots} of processes, which generalise the $n$-th
roots of radical fault tolerance and replace its divisibility obstruction by a
symmetry condition in which the stabiliser of a component plays the part of
its replication factor. A final section lets decorations be words in
received data, so that a process can undo a frame it has measured. This
yields frame-fixing, which corrects drift without redundancy, and a
drift-tolerant triple-redundancy decoder that also corrects one skew or
erasure. The price is the word problem for $G$, which every congruence check
must now solve.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Earlier work treated the syntax of the rho calculus as a type constructor
parametric in a type $D$ of data carried by names and messages, and settled on
a pattern-matching binder that lets a receiver bind every component of what it
receives, so that the datum on a message is no longer observationally
inert~\cite{wsh}. That construction asks nothing of $D$. This note asks what
the calculus gains when $D$ is a group.

The answer begins with an action. A group acts on itself by left
multiplication, and that action lifts to processes by multiplying the datum of
every name a process mentions, at every depth of quotation. The lifted action
is free on names: no non-identity element fixes a name. Freeness turns
questions about the symmetry of a process into counting arguments, and the
calculus acquires a notion of \emph{orientation}. The same channel code may
occur at many orientations, and a rotation of a process moves all of them at
once.

Two further ingredients complete the setting. The first is a constant
$\chaos$ for chaos: the process that may do anything, so that composing
anything with it adds nothing. The second is a summation, which the
communication rule needs because its reduct is a sum. The rule itself is the
point of the construction. A receive on one orientation of a channel and a
send on another communicate, and the continuation is delivered twice, once
transported by the relative orientation of the two ends and once by its
inverse. When the two ends agree, the transports cancel and the ordinary rho
communication is recovered.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The calculus $\calc$ (Section~\ref{sec:calculus}): syntax, the free
  action, chaos, idempotent summation, matching, and the oriented COMM rule.
\item Basic observations (Section~\ref{sec:obs}): the action is an
  automorphism; chaos has every name free; substitution commutes with the
  action and matching is invariant under it; reduction is $G$-equivariant
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:equivariance}), and so are context-labelled transitions;
  the calculus is conservative over the original; and an orbit-counting
  theorem for open and closed terms
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:counting}) with a crystallographic corollary for rotation
  groups (Corollary~\ref{cor:crystal}).
\item Three applications: symmetry reduction with symmetry computed from the
  term (Section~\ref{sec:reduction}); symmetric rings as orbit products
  (Section~\ref{sec:rings}); and twisted roots of processes, with a
  divisibility theorem that interpolates between replication and sharding
  (Section~\ref{sec:roots}). The unique decomposition it needs holds up to
  structural congruence and is proved, not assumed
  (Lemma~\ref{lem:upd}).
\item An extension with dynamic frames, and error correction built on it
  (Section~\ref{sec:ecc}): frame-fixing, a worked triple-redundancy decoder
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:tmr}), and an account of the cost of the word problem
  that the extension imposes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.}
Summation is taken idempotent throughout, and chaos absorbs it. Other readings
of summation are possible and are not pursued here.

\section{The calculus}\label{sec:calculus}

\subsection{Syntax}

Fix a group $(G,\cdot,e,(-)^{-1})$ and a set $V$ of variables, divided into
process variables $X,Y,\dots$ and data variables $\delta,\delta',\dots$. We
write $e$ for the identity of $G$, reserving $\chaos$ for chaos. The terms of
$\calc$ are
\begin{align*}
d &\;::=\; g \;\mid\; \fr{g}{\delta}
  && g\in G \quad\text{(data)}\\
x,u,\pi &\;::=\; \qn{P}{d}
  && \text{(names; patterns)}\\
P,Q &\;::=\; \nil \;\mid\; \chaos \;\mid\; \fr{g}{X} \;\mid\; \rcv{\pi}{x}{P}
  \;\mid\; \out{x}{Q}{d} \;\mid\; P \mid Q \;\mid\; P + Q
  && \text{(processes)}
\end{align*}
Variables occur \emph{decorated}: $\fr{g}{X}$ and $\fr{g}{\delta}$ carry a
group element, the action that is pending on the variable until it is
instantiated. We write $X$ for $\fr{e}{X}$ and $\delta$ for $\fr{e}{\delta}$.
A name $\qn{P}{d}$ pairs a \emph{code} $P$ with a datum $d$; we write
$\code(\qn{P}{d}) = P$ and $\nu(\qn{P}{d}) = d$. A message
$\out{x}{Q}{d}$ ships the pair $\langle Q, d\rangle$ on $x$; at communication
it is received as the name $\qn{Q}{d}$.

In a receive $\rcv{\pi}{x}{P}$ the pattern $\pi$ is a name in which every
variable occurring is bound by the receive, and each occurs exactly once,
possibly decorated. The
canonical pattern $\qn{X}{\delta}$ binds both the code and the datum of what
is received. As in~\cite{wsh}, process variables play the part the drop
operator plays in the original calculus~\cite{rho}: a received code is used by
writing its variable in process position. A name is \emph{closed} when it
contains no variables, and a process is closed when every variable in it is
bound. Reduction is defined on closed processes.

\begin{remark}[Notation]
The sketch that motivated this note wrote the action with $*$. Since $*$ is
the drop operator in the rho calculus, we write the action as $\act{g}{P}$.
For the same reason the identity of $G$ is $e$, leaving $\chaos$ free for
chaos.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The free action}

\begin{definition}[Action]\label{def:action}
For $g\in G$ define $\act{g}{-}$ on data, names and processes by
\begin{align*}
\act{g}{h} &= gh \quad (h\in G) &
\act{g}{\fr{k}{\delta}} &= \fr{gk}{\delta} &
\act{g}{\fr{k}{X}} &= \fr{gk}{X}\\
\act{g}{\nil} &= \nil &
\act{g}{\chaos} &= \chaos &
\act{g}{\qn{P}{d}} &= \qn{\act{g}{P}}{\act{g}{d}}
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\act{g}{(P\mid Q)} &= \act{g}{P}\mid\act{g}{Q} &
\act{g}{(P+Q)} &= \act{g}{P}+\act{g}{Q}\\
\act{g}{\rcv{\pi}{x}{P}} &= \rcv{\act{g}{\pi}}{\act{g}{x}}{\act{g}{P}} &
\act{g}{\out{x}{Q}{d}} &= \out{\act{g}{x}}{\act{g}{Q}}{\act{g}{d}}
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

The action multiplies every literal datum on the left, at every depth of
quotation, inside patterns as well as in names and messages. At a variable it
is not applied but recorded, and pending actions compose,
$\act{h}{\fr{k}{X}}=\fr{hk}{X}$, so each occurrence carries a single element.
The record is discharged by substitution:
\[
(\fr{g}{X})\sigma = \act{g}{\sigma(X)},
\qquad
(\fr{g}{\delta})\sigma = g\,\sigma(\delta).
\]
The action renames nothing, so it needs no side condition against capture.
On closed terms it is the image of left multiplication $L_g : G\to G$ under
the functor that the parametric syntax defines.

\paragraph{Local frames.}
A decoration has a reading. A bound variable holds what was received in the
receiver's own frame, and its decoration records the transformation from that
frame to the frame in which the body is written. A rotated copy of a receive
therefore binds exactly what the unrotated one binds, and the rotation is
applied when the variable is used. Changing the frame of a bound variable
uniformly, at its binding occurrence and in the body, changes nothing
observable; this is the axiom (FRAME) below.

\subsection{Structural congruence}

Structural congruence $\scong$ is the least congruence, closed under every
term former including quotation, that satisfies the axioms of
Table~\ref{tab:scong}. Two names are equal when their codes are structurally
congruent and their data are equal.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
(PAR) & $P\mid\nil \scong P$,\; $P\mid Q\scong Q\mid P$,\;
        $(P\mid Q)\mid R\scong P\mid(Q\mid R)$ & monoid\\
(SUM) & $P+Q\scong Q+P$,\; $(P+Q)+R\scong P+(Q+R)$,\; $P+P\scong P$
      & semilattice\\
(DIST) & $P\mid(Q+R)\scong P\mid Q + P\mid R$ & distributivity\\
(CHAOS) & $\chaos\mid P\scong\chaos$,\; $\chaos + P \scong \chaos$ &
  chaos absorbs both\\
(FRAME) & $\rcv{\pi}{x}{P}\scong\rcv{\pi^{Z,h}}{x}{P^{Z,h}}$ &
  change of frame\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Axioms of structural congruence.}
\label{tab:scong}
\end{table}

In (FRAME), $Z$ is a variable bound by $\pi$, $h\in G$, and $T^{Z,h}$
replaces every occurrence $\fr{k}{Z}$ in $T$ by $\fr{kh}{Z}$: right
multiplication of the decorations of one bound variable. It is a frame
$\alpha$-conversion. A matcher for $\pi^{Z,h}$ binds $Z$ to $h^{-1}$ times
what a matcher for $\pi$ binds, and the body occurrences, multiplied by $h$ on
the right, discharge to the same result. Every receive is congruent to one
whose binding occurrences are undecorated, so closed terms are identified
exactly as they would be if the action left variables alone.

The axiom $\chaos\mid P\scong\chaos$ is the defining property of chaos: it
represents all possible behaviours, so composing it with any $P$ cannot
enrich it. The same reasoning motivates $\chaos+P\scong\chaos$. The two are
consistent with (DIST):
$(\chaos+P)\mid Q\scong\chaos\mid Q + P\mid Q\scong\chaos$. Note that $\nil$ is
not a unit for $+$; it is the inert configuration, one alternative among
others.

By (DIST), every process is congruent to a finite sum of sum-free
\emph{configurations}, or to $\chaos$. Summation is therefore an ensemble of
alternatives, and reduction (below) refines one member of the ensemble.

\subsection{Matching}

Matching is performed on normal forms. Orient (CHAOS) and (DIST) from left to
right, remove duplicate summands, and use (FRAME) to make every binding
occurrence undecorated; write $\nf(P)$ for a normal form of $P$ modulo
associativity and commutativity of $\mid$ and $+$ (AC). A substitution
$\sigma$ maps process variables to closed processes and data variables to
elements of $G$, and acts on decorated occurrences as above.

\begin{definition}[Matching]\label{def:match}
For a pattern $\pi$ and a closed name $n$,
\[
\match(\pi,n) \;=\; \{\,\sigma \;:\; \pi\sigma =_{AC} \nf(n)\,\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Matching modulo AC can have several solutions, and a communication may use any
of them. A decorated pattern variable is solved by inversion: $\fr{g}{X}$
matches $Q$ exactly with $\sigma(X)=\act{g^{-1}}{Q}$, and $\fr{g}{\delta}$
matches $d$ with $\sigma(\delta)=g^{-1}d$. This is where the group axioms enter
the definition of communication itself: under a monoid action a pending
decoration could leave a pattern with no solution or with many.

One further convention in the definition is deliberate.

\begin{remark}[Chaos in quotes]\label{rem:chaosmatch}
Matching is \emph{not} performed modulo (CHAOS). Otherwise the pattern
$\qn{X\mid Y}{\delta}$ would match $\qn{\chaos}{d}$ under every substitution
$\{\chaos/X, Q/Y\}$, since $\chaos\mid Q\scong\chaos$ for all $Q$, and matching
against quoted chaos would not be finitary. In normal form chaos is an atom:
it matches a bare process variable and nothing composite.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Reduction}

The \emph{relative orientation} of a receive on $x$ and a send on $u$ is
$\nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1}$. The reduction relation $\red$ on closed processes is the
least relation closed under the rules of Table~\ref{tab:red}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
(COMM) & $\dfrac{\code(x)\scong\code(u) \qquad \sigma\in\match(\pi,\qn{Q}{d})}
  {\rcv{\pi}{x}{P}\mid\out{u}{Q}{d}\;\red\;
   \act{(\nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1})}{P\sigma} \;+\;
   \act{(\nu(u)\nu(x)^{-1})}{P\sigma}}$\\
(PAR) & $\dfrac{P\red P'}{P\mid R\red P'\mid R}$
\qquad (SUM) \; $\dfrac{P\red P'}{P+R\red P'+R}$\\
(STRUCT) & $\dfrac{P\scong P' \qquad P'\red Q' \qquad Q'\scong Q}{P\red Q}$
\qquad (DIV) \; $\chaos\red\chaos$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Reduction rules.}
\label{tab:red}
\end{table}

The premise $\code(x)\scong\code(u)$ is the \emph{gate} of the rule. A receive
and a send interact when they are on the same channel code, possibly at
different orientations, and the relative orientation then acts on the
continuation. The two coefficients are mutually inverse, so writing
$h = \nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1}$ the reduct is $\act{h}{P\sigma}+\act{h^{-1}}{P\sigma}$.

\begin{remark}[The gate]\label{rem:gate}
Other gates are possible.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item The \emph{strict} gate $x = u$ makes every coefficient $e$, and the
  calculus collapses to the original one extended by chaos and summation.
\item The \emph{open} gate, always true, lets every receive meet every send
  whose message matches its pattern. Pattern matching is then the only
  selector of partners, and channel discipline is lost.
\item A \emph{$K$-gate}, $\code(x)\scong\code(u)$ and
  $\nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1}\in K$ for a subset $K\subseteq G$ that contains $e$,
  is closed under inverses and is closed under conjugation, restricts
  interaction between orientations of a channel to the Cayley graph of $G$
  with connection set $K$.
\end{itemize}
All of the results below hold for each of these gates; we fix the coded gate
as the default because it is the least change that lets orientation matter.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Order of the second coefficient]\label{rem:order}
The sketch that motivated this note used $\nu(x)^{-1}\nu(u)$ as the second
coefficient. In an abelian group it coincides with $\nu(u)\nu(x)^{-1}$. In a
non-abelian group it is the $\nu(x)$-conjugate of the inverse of the first
coefficient, and with that choice reduction is equivariant only for elements
commuting with $\nu(x)^{-1}\nu(u)$; see Remark~\ref{rem:nonabelian}. The choice
$\nu(u)\nu(x)^{-1}$ is the one that makes reduction equivariant for every
group, and in particular for non-abelian rotation groups such as $SO(3)$.
\end{remark}

\section{Basic observations}\label{sec:obs}

\subsection{The action is an automorphism}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:auto}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\act{e}{P}=P$ and $\act{g}{(\act{h}{P})}=\act{(gh)}{P}$.
\item $P\scong Q$ if and only if $\act{g}{P}\scong\act{g}{Q}$.
\item $\nf(\act{g}{P}) =_{AC} \act{g}{\nf(P)}$.
\end{enumerate}
So each $\act{g}{-}$ is an automorphism of
$(\Proc/{\scong},\mid,+,\nil,\chaos)$, and $G$ acts on processes up to
structural congruence.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) is by induction on terms; the base cases are a literal datum and a
decorated variable, where $g(hk)=(gh)k$. For (ii), the action maps every
instance of an axiom in Table~\ref{tab:scong} to an instance of the same
axiom, because it commutes with the term formers and fixes $\nil$ and
$\chaos$; for (FRAME) this is the commutation of left and right multiplication. Hence it preserves
$\scong$, and $\act{g^{-1}}{-}$ is its inverse by (i). (iii) holds because the
oriented normalisation rules are themselves invariant under the action.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Chaos has every name}

Call a name occurrence in $P$ \emph{free} when every variable in it is free
in $P$; for closed $P$ these are the closed names. The \emph{free atoms}
$\FA(P)$ of $P$ are its free names in subject position or in a payload,
together with its free variable occurrences $\fr{k}{X}$ in process position:
\begin{align*}
\FA(\nil)&=\emptyset, &
\FA(\fr{k}{X})&=\{\fr{k}{X}\},\\
\FA(P\mid Q)&=\FA(P)\cup\FA(Q), &
\FA(P+Q)&=\FA(P)\cup\FA(Q),\\
\FA(\out{x}{Q}{d})&=\{x\}^{\circ}\cup\FA(Q), &
\FA(\rcv{\pi}{x}{P})&=\{x\}^{\circ}\cup(\FA(P)\setminus\mathrm{bound}(\pi)),
\end{align*}
where $\{x\}^{\circ}$ is $\{x\}$ if the occurrence is free and empty otherwise,
and $\FA(P)\setminus\mathrm{bound}(\pi)$ discards atoms mentioning a variable
bound by $\pi$. Write $\FN(P)$ for the names among the free atoms, and
$\Atoms$ for the set of all atoms. $\FA(\chaos)$ is fixed by the following
proposition.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:chaosnames}
If $\FA$ respects $\scong$ and is a union over $\mid$, then
$\FA(\chaos)=\Atoms$: chaos has every name, and every variable, free.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any name $x$, $\FA(\chaos)=\FA(\chaos\mid\out{x}{\nil}{e})
=\FA(\chaos)\cup\{x\}$, so $x\in\FA(\chaos)$; likewise with $\fr{k}{X}$ in
place of the send.
\end{proof}

Chaos has every name free because it is absorbing. Two consequences are worth
recording at once. First, $\act{g}{\chaos}=\chaos$ is no longer an axiom about
the action but an instance of its definition, since left multiplication
permutes $\Atoms$. Second, no name is fresh for chaos: an argument that picks
a channel not free in $P$ fails as soon as $P\scong\chaos$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fnaction}
$\FA(\act{g}{P})=\act{g}{\FA(P)}$, and $\FA$ respects $\scong$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first claim is by induction: the action preserves freeness of
occurrences and permutes $\Atoms$. The second is a check of the axioms. For
(CHAOS), $\FA(\chaos\mid P)=\Atoms\cup\FA(P)=\Atoms=\FA(\chaos)$; (FRAME)
changes only bound variables, which free atoms do not mention.
\end{proof}

Without deferral this would fail in an instructive way. If the action left
variables alone, a bare variable $X$ and a name $\qn{X}{\delta}$ built only
from variables would be fixed by the whole group, and freeness would hold only
for closed names. Deferral makes every atom move.

\subsection{Substitution and matching}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:subst}
$\act{g}{(P\sigma)}=(\act{g}{P})\sigma$, and
$\match(\act{g}{\pi},\act{g}{n})=\match(\pi,n)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The substitution identity is by induction on $P$. At a decorated process
variable,
$(\act{g}{\fr{k}{X}})\sigma=(\fr{gk}{X})\sigma=\act{(gk)}{\sigma(X)}
=\act{g}{(\fr{k}{X})\sigma}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}(i), and a decorated data
variable is the same calculation in $G$. At a literal datum both sides give
$gh$. For matching, $\pi\sigma=_{AC}\nf(n)$ if and only if
$\act{g}{(\pi\sigma)}=_{AC}\act{g}{\nf(n)}$, because the action is a bijection
that respects AC; by the substitution identity and
Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}(iii) this reads
$(\act{g}{\pi})\sigma=_{AC}\nf(\act{g}{n})$.
\end{proof}

The action passes through substitution without touching it, and a rotated
redex is matched by the \emph{same} substitution as the original. For example,
$\qn{X}{h}$ matches $\qn{Q}{d}$ exactly when $h=d$, with $X\mapsto Q$; after
the action it is $\qn{\fr{g}{X}}{gh}$, which matches $\qn{\act{g}{Q}}{gd}$
exactly when $gh=gd$, again with $X\mapsto Q$. Acting on patterns is what keeps
a rotated redex a redex, and deferral is what keeps its matcher fixed.

\subsection{Equivariance}

\begin{theorem}[Equivariance]\label{thm:equivariance}
If $P\red P'$ then $\act{g}{P}\red\act{g}{P'}$, for every $g\in G$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on the derivation. (PAR), (SUM) and (STRUCT) follow from
Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}, and (DIV) from $\act{g}{\chaos}=\chaos$. For (COMM), let
$a=\nu(x)$ and $b=\nu(u)$. The rotated redex is
$\rcv{\act{g}{\pi}}{\act{g}{x}}{\act{g}{P}}\mid\out{\act{g}{u}}{\act{g}{Q}}{gd}$.
Its gate holds because $\code(\act{g}{x})=\act{g}{\code(x)}$ and
Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}(ii). By Lemma~\ref{lem:subst}, $\sigma$ itself is a
matcher of the rotated redex, and the continuation becomes
$(\act{g}{P})\sigma=\act{g}{(P\sigma)}$. The rotated data are $ga$
and $gb$, so the coefficients are
\[
(ga)(gb)^{-1}=g\,ab^{-1}g^{-1},
\qquad
(gb)(ga)^{-1}=g\,ba^{-1}g^{-1}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}(i) the reduct is
\[
\act{(g\,ab^{-1}g^{-1})}{\act{g}{(P\sigma)}}
+\act{(g\,ba^{-1}g^{-1})}{\act{g}{(P\sigma)}}
=\act{g}{\bigl(\act{(ab^{-1})}{P\sigma}+\act{(ba^{-1})}{P\sigma}\bigr)},
\]
which is $g$ applied to the original reduct.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The non-abelian case]\label{rem:nonabelian}
With $a^{-1}b$ as the second coefficient, the rotated coefficient is
$(ga)^{-1}(gb)=a^{-1}b$, unchanged, and the second summand becomes
$\act{(a^{-1}b\,g)}{P\sigma}$ where equivariance needs
$\act{(g\,a^{-1}b)}{P\sigma}$. These agree for all $P$ only when $g$ commutes
with $a^{-1}b$. Equivariance is therefore what selects the coefficients.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Contexts and labels.}
A context $C$ is a term with one hole, which may sit under receives that bind
variables of what fills it. Let $\act{g}{C}$ act on everything but the hole.
By induction on $C$,
\[
(\act{g}{C})[\act{g}{P}] \;=\; \act{g}{(C[P])},
\]
for open $P$ as well as closed: a variable of $P$ captured by a pattern of $C$
is decorated on both sides by the same element. Write
$P\xrightarrow{C}P'$ when $C[P]\red P'$, the transitions of context-labelled
bisimulation.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:labels}
$P\xrightarrow{C}P'$ if and only if
$\act{g}{P}\xrightarrow{\act{g}{C}}\act{g}{P'}$. Consequently, if a set of
observing contexts $\mathcal{O}$ is closed under the action, then the largest
$\mathcal{O}$-bisimulation $\approx_{\mathcal{O}}$ is equivariant:
$P\approx_{\mathcal{O}}Q$ if and only if
$\act{g}{P}\approx_{\mathcal{O}}\act{g}{Q}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:equivariance} applied to $C[P]$, with
$g^{-1}$ for the converse. For the second, if $\mathcal{R}$ is an
$\mathcal{O}$-bisimulation then so is
$\{(\act{g}{P},\act{g}{Q}) : (P,Q)\in\mathcal{R}\}$, by the first claim and
closure of $\mathcal{O}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Conservativity and frames}

\begin{proposition}[Conservativity]\label{prop:conservative}
If $\nu(x)=\nu(u)$, in particular if $x=u$, then
$\rcv{\pi}{x}{P}\mid\out{u}{Q}{d}\red P\sigma$. Hence for the trivial group,
and on any process whose communications occur between equal names, $\calc$
reduces exactly as the pattern-matching calculus of~\cite{wsh}, extended by
chaos and summation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both coefficients are $e$, so the reduct is $P\sigma+P\sigma\scong P\sigma$ by
idempotence.
\end{proof}

This is the cancellation in the rule. The relative orientation of matched ends
is the identity, and the doubling of the continuation disappears into the
idempotence of summation.

\paragraph{Symmetry between the ends.}
Exchanging the roles of $\nu(x)$ and $\nu(u)$ exchanges the two summands of the
reduct, which is unchanged. The rule does not depend on whose frame the
relative orientation is measured in.

\paragraph{Frames.}
Consider a name $n$ in the receiver's frame, $\nu(n)=\nu(x)$. The second summand
moves it to $\nu(u)\nu(x)^{-1}\nu(x)=\nu(u)$: into the sender's frame. The first
moves it to $\nu(x)\nu(u)^{-1}\nu(x)$, the reflection of the sender's frame
through the receiver's; in an abelian group written additively, a receiver at
$j$ hearing from $k$ lands at $k$ in one summand and at $2j-k$ in the other.
The bound variables of the continuation move with it: what was received is
held in the receiver's frame, and the coefficient is applied to it when it is
used, by Lemma~\ref{lem:subst}.

\paragraph{Anisotropic and isotropic codes.}
A code $c$ is \emph{isotropic} for $H\le G$ when $\act{h}{c}\scong c$ for all
$h\in H$; nameless codes such as $\nil$ are isotropic for all of $G$. All
orientations $\qn{c}{h}$ of an isotropic code share a code, so under the coded
gate they communicate with one another, with non-trivial coefficients. A code
with names is \emph{anisotropic}: rotating a name moves its code as well as
its datum, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:counting} below only finitely many rotations
can fix it. Anisotropic codes therefore produce genuinely distinct channels
under rotation, and processes built from them reduce exactly as in the
original calculus (Proposition~\ref{prop:conservative}). Rotation can thus be
used either to \emph{place} copies of a process on distinct channels, or to
\emph{orient} a single channel.

\subsection{Stabilisers and orbit counting}

\begin{definition}
The \emph{symmetry group} of $P$ is
$\Stab(P)=\{\,g\in G : \act{g}{P}\scong P\,\}$.
\end{definition}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}, $\Stab(P)$ is a subgroup,
$\Stab(\act{g}{P})=g\,\Stab(P)\,g^{-1}$,
$\Stab(P\mid Q)\supseteq\Stab(P)\cap\Stab(Q)$, and
$\Stab(\nil)=\Stab(\chaos)=G$.

Every atom has a \emph{frame} $\phi$: for a name, $\phi(\qn{Q}{h})=h$ and
$\phi(\qn{Q}{\fr{k}{\delta}})=k$; for a variable, $\phi(\fr{k}{X})=k$. The
action multiplies frames, $\phi(\act{g}{a})=g\,\phi(a)$.

\begin{theorem}[Orbit counting]\label{thm:counting}
$\Stab(P)$ acts freely on $\FA(P)$. Consequently, if $\FA(P)$ is finite, then
either $\FA(P)=\emptyset$ and $\Stab(P)=G$, or $\Stab(P)$ is finite and
$|\Stab(P)|$ divides $|\FA(P)|$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $g\in\Stab(P)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:fnaction} gives
$\act{g}{\FA(P)}=\FA(\act{g}{P})=\FA(P)$, so $\Stab(P)$ acts on $\FA(P)$. The
action is free: $\act{g}{a}=a$ requires $g\,\phi(a)=\phi(a)$, hence $g=e$.
If $\FA(P)$ is finite and non-empty, every orbit of $\Stab(P)$ on it has
$|\Stab(P)|$ elements, so $\Stab(P)$ is finite and its order divides
$|\FA(P)|$. If $\FA(P)=\emptyset$, then $P$ contains no receive, no send, no
free variable and no chaos outside quotation, so it is built from $\nil$ and
the two operators and every $g$ fixes it.
\end{proof}

$\FA(P)$ is finite exactly when $\chaos$ does not occur in $P$ outside
quotation, and the theorem applies to open terms as well as closed ones. So
it covers every process that is not chaotic, and chaos is the one way for a
process with atoms to be fixed by an infinite group. For closed $P$ the free
atoms are the free names.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:infinite}
If $G$ is infinite and $\Stab(P)=G$, then $\FA(P)=\emptyset$ or
$\FA(P)=\Atoms$. In words: the only processes invariant under an infinite
group are those that mention no names or free variables, and those that
contain chaos.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Symmetry is computable]\label{prop:computable}
If $\FA(P)$ is finite and non-empty, fix $a_0\in\FA(P)$. Then
\[
\Stab(P)\subseteq\{\,\phi(a)\,\phi(a_0)^{-1} : a\in\FA(P)\,\},
\]
so $\Stab(P)$ is found by at most $|\FA(P)|$ congruence checks, for any group,
finite or not.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $g\in\Stab(P)$ then $\act{g}{a_0}\in\FA(P)$, and
$\phi(\act{g}{a_0})=g\,\phi(a_0)$ determines $g$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Rotations}

Take the data to be orientations. With $G=SO(2)$ a name is a channel code at a
planar orientation, and with $G=SO(3)$ at an orientation in space.

\begin{corollary}[Crystallographic restriction]\label{cor:crystal}
Let $\FA(P)$ be finite and non-empty.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $G=SO(2)$, then $\Stab(P)$ is cyclic of order dividing $|\FA(P)|$.
\item If $G=SO(3)$, then $\Stab(P)$ is cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral,
  octahedral or icosahedral, of order dividing $|\FA(P)|$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:counting} the stabiliser is a finite subgroup, and these
are the finite subgroups of $SO(2)$ and $SO(3)$~\cite{armstrong}.
\end{proof}

A process can have the rotational symmetry of a polygon or a polyhedron, but
never continuous rotational symmetry unless it mentions no names or is
chaotic. An icosahedrally symmetric process mentions a multiple of $60$ names.

\subsection{Orbit products and orbit sums}

For a finite subgroup $H\le G$ and a process $R$ define
\[
\textstyle\prod_H R \;=\; \big|_{h\in H}\;\act{h}{R},
\qquad
\sum_H R \;=\; \sum_{h\in H}\;\act{h}{R}.
\]
Both are $H$-invariant: $\act{k}{\prod_H R}$ re-indexes the product by
$h\mapsto kh$, and associativity and commutativity of $\mid$ do the rest; the
same holds for $\sum_H R$. Orbit products are the standard way to impose a
symmetry on a process, with $R$ playing the part of a fundamental domain. The
reduct of (COMM) is a partial orbit sum, over the two-element set
$\{h,h^{-1}\}$, and is invariant under the inversion that exchanges them.

\section{Application I: symmetry reduction}\label{sec:reduction}

Explicit-state exploration of concurrent systems is limited by the size of the
state space, and symmetry reduction is one of the standard
remedies~\cite{emerson-sistla,ip-dill,clarke-sym}: when a group acts on the
states by automorphisms of the transition relation, it suffices to explore one
representative per orbit. In those treatments the symmetry is a property of
a particular system and must be declared or detected. In $\calc$ it is a
property of the calculus.

\begin{theorem}[Orbit quotient]\label{thm:quotient}
Let $H\le G$. Define $[P]=\{\act{h}{P'} : h\in H,\, P'\scong P\}$ and
$[P]\red_H[Q]$ if $P\red Q'$ for some $Q'\in[Q]$. Then $\red_H$ is
well-defined, and the quotient map $P\mapsto[P]$ is a functional
bisimulation from $(\Proc,\red)$ to $(\Proc/H,\red_H)$. Consequently, for any
set of states $\Phi$ closed under $\scong$ and under the action of $H$, a state
in $\Phi$ is reachable from $P_0$ if and only if an orbit in $\Phi/H$ is
reachable from $[P_0]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Well-definedness: if $\act{h}{P}\red Q$ then $P\red\act{h^{-1}}{Q}$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:equivariance}, and $\act{h^{-1}}{Q}\in[Q]$. The bisimulation
clauses are then immediate: every step of $P$ projects to a step of $[P]$, and
every step of $[P]$ is the projection of a step of $P$ by the same argument.
\end{proof}

Three features distinguish this from the usual setting.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Soundness for every subgroup.} Equivariance holds for the whole
  calculus, not for a given system, so the quotient is sound for any $H$ and
  any initial process. Barbs move under the action, $\act{h}{P}\downarrow
  \act{h}{x}$ whenever $P\downarrow x$, so the invariant observations are
  orbit barbs $P\downarrow H{\cdot}x$, and for a spatial logic whose formulae mention names, with the action extended
  to formulae, satisfaction is equivariant: $\act{g}{P}\models\act{g}{\varphi}$
  whenever $P\models\varphi$. Invariant formulae can be checked on the quotient.
\item \emph{Effectiveness from the term.} The quotient pays when the
  reachable set is closed under $H$, which holds when $H\le\Stab(P_0)$: then
  $P_0\red^*P$ gives $P_0\scong\act{h}{P_0}\red^*\act{h}{P}$. By
  Proposition~\ref{prop:computable} the largest such $H$ is computed from
  $P_0$ with $|\FN(P_0)|$ checks; nothing is declared.
\item \emph{A bound.} By Theorem~\ref{thm:counting}, $|\Stab(P_0)|$ divides
  $|\FN(P_0)|$. The symmetry that can be extracted from the initial state is
  bounded by its number of free names, and the reduction factor on any orbit
  is at most $|H|$.
\end{enumerate}

Canonicalisation, the choice of a representative of $[P]$, costs at most
$|H|$ congruence-class comparisons for finite $H$, by taking the least of
$\{\act{h}{P}\}_{h\in H}$ in any total order on normal forms. A spatial logic
model checker for the rho calculus, such as the port of SLMC~\cite{slmc}, is
the natural home for this reduction, since spatial formulae already range over
the structure that the action permutes.

\section{Application II: symmetric rings}\label{sec:rings}

Rings of identical agents are the classical setting for symmetry in
distributed computing. Orbit products give them a uniform syntax.

\subsection{The construction}

Let $r\in G$ have order $n$: a generator of $\mathbb{Z}/n$, or the rotation by
$2\pi/n$ in $SO(2)$. Choose two anisotropic codes, for instance
\[
a_f=\out{\qn{\nil}{e}}{\nil}{e},
\qquad
a_t=\rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{\qn{\nil}{e}}{\nil},
\]
and base names $f=\qn{a_f}{e}$ (a fork) and $t=\qn{a_t}{e}$ (a table, on which
eating is announced). Write $f_k=\act{r^k}{f}$ and $t_k=\act{r^k}{t}$; indices
are taken mod $n$. Since $\act{r^k}{a_f}=\out{\qn{\nil}{r^k}}{\nil}{r^k}$, the
codes of $f_0,\dots,f_{n-1}$ are pairwise distinct, so the forks are $n$
distinct channels and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:conservative}, every
communication below is an ordinary one. Let
\begin{align*}
\mathit{Phil} &= \rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{f_0}{\rcv{\qn{Y}{\delta'}}{f_1}{\bigl(
  \out{t_0}{\nil}{e}\mid\out{f_0}{X}{\delta}\mid\out{f_1}{Y}{\delta'}\bigr)}},\\
\mathit{Fork} &= \out{f_0}{\nil}{e},
\qquad
R=\mathit{Phil}\mid\mathit{Fork},
\qquad
\ring_n=\textstyle\prod_{\langle r\rangle}R.
\end{align*}
The copy $\act{r^k}{R}$ is the $k$-th philosopher, who takes fork $f_k$ and
then fork $f_{k+1}$, announces on $t_k$, and returns both forks, together with
fork $f_k$ itself, carrying the token $\langle\nil,r^k\rangle$. In the
$k$-th philosopher the pattern is $\qn{\fr{r^k}{X}}{\fr{r^k}{\delta}}$. It
still matches every token, and it binds what it receives in the philosopher's
own frame: every philosopher runs literally the same body, in its own
coordinates.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ring}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\Stab(\ring_n)=\langle r\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/n$ when $G=\mathbb{Z}/n$
  or $G=SO(2)$.
\item $\ring_n$ reduces in $n$ steps to the deadlocked state
  $D_n=\prod_{\langle r\rangle}W$, where
  $W=\rcv{\qn{Y}{\delta'}}{f_1}{\bigl(\out{t_0}{\nil}{e}\mid
  \out{f_0}{\nil}{e}\mid\out{f_1}{Y}{\delta'}\bigr)}$.
  The state $D_n$ has no reduct, has no barb on any $t_k$, and
  $\Stab(D_n)=\langle r\rangle$.
\item For $n\ge2$, $\ring_n$ also reaches states with a barb on some $t_k$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) The product is $\langle r\rangle$-invariant. Conversely
$\FN(\ring_n)=\{f_k,t_k\}_{k}$ has $2n$ elements. A symmetry maps fork names
to fork names, since a send code is never congruent to a receive code, so
by Proposition~\ref{prop:computable} it is $\nu(f_k)\nu(f_0)^{-1}=r^k$.
(ii) For each $k$ in turn, philosopher $k$ receives the token of fork $k$; the
coefficients are $e$; solving the pattern
$\qn{\fr{r^k}{X}}{\fr{r^k}{\delta}}$ against $\qn{\nil}{r^k}$ gives
$\sigma=\{\nil/X,\,e/\delta\}$, the same matcher for every $k$, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:subst} the continuation is $\act{r^k}{W}$. After $n$ such steps no send on any $f_k$ remains, so no
(COMM) applies. Its symmetry group is computed as in (i). (iii) Let
philosopher $0$ take $f_0$ and then $f_1$ before philosopher $1$ moves.
\end{proof}

The $n$ steps to $D_n$ are a single step and its $n-1$ rotations: the $k$-th is
$\act{r^k}{}$ of the $0$-th, placed in context. Equivariance is exactly the
property that the adversary of Lehmann and Rabin exploits~\cite{lehmann-rabin}:
from a symmetric state, a scheduler that lets every copy take the
corresponding step returns the system to a symmetric state, and a deterministic
symmetric protocol cannot leave the symmetric states without help. Their
theorem, that no deterministic, fully distributed and symmetric solution to the
dining philosophers exists, can therefore be stated inside $\calc$ with
$\langle r\rangle$-invariance as the formal notion of symmetry. Its proof in
the calculus is left to further work, together with the question of which
ingredient of $\calc$ is the symmetry breaker that their randomised solution
requires. With summation read as an idempotent ensemble, as here,
$\mathit{Phil}_{L}+\mathit{Phil}_{R}$ is demonic choice rather than a coin, and
the deadlocked configuration remains in the ensemble.

\begin{remark}[Isotropic rings]
If the anisotropic code $a_f$ is replaced by the isotropic code $\nil$, the
names $\qn{\nil}{r^k}$ all share a code. Under the coded gate every orientation
then communicates with every other, and a receive at $r^j$ from a send at
$r^k$ transports the names of its own frame to $r^k$ in one summand and to
$r^{2j-k}$ in the other. Under the $K$-gate with $K=\{e,r,r^{-1}\}$ only neighbours
communicate, and the interaction topology is the Cayley graph of
$\mathbb{Z}/n$. Rings of isotropic channels are therefore not rings of fixed
agents but of agents that move as they communicate.
\end{remark}

\section{Application III: twisted roots}\label{sec:roots}

The note on radical fault tolerance~\cite{rft} studies $n$-th roots of
processes, $R$ with $R^n\approx P$, where $R^n$ is the $n$-fold parallel
composition. Under a unique-decomposition hypothesis, which there concerns a
behavioural equivalence, the existence of roots is governed by divisibility: every prime component of $P$ must occur with
multiplicity divisible by $n$, so that a component occurring once has no
root. The group action offers a second way to divide a process into $n$ equal
parts: into $n$ copies of one process at different orientations.

\begin{definition}
Let $H\le G$ be finite. A process $R$ is an \emph{$H$-twisted root} of $P$ if
$\prod_H R\scong P$.
\end{definition}

If $H\le\Stab(R)$ the twist is trivial, $\prod_H R\scong R^{|H|}$, and an
untwisted root is recovered. At the other extreme, if $f_k=\act{r^k}{f}$ as in
Section~\ref{sec:rings}, the process
\[
P=\out{f_0}{\nil}{e}\mid\out{f_1}{\nil}{r}\mid\cdots\mid
\out{f_{n-1}}{\nil}{r^{n-1}}
\]
has every prime component with multiplicity one and so no $n$-th root, yet
$\out{f_0}{\nil}{e}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}/n$-twisted root of it.

To say exactly when twisted roots exist we need unique decomposition. Twisted
roots are defined up to structural congruence, and there, unlike up to
bisimilarity, unique decomposition is a lemma rather than a hypothesis. Call a
closed process \emph{flat} if neither $+$ nor $\chaos$ occurs in it outside
prefixes and quotations. A \emph{prime} is a process not congruent to $\nil$
and not congruent to a composition of two processes neither of which is
$\nil$.

\begin{lemma}[Unique decomposition up to $\scong$]\label{lem:upd}
Every flat process is congruent to a parallel composition of receives and
sends, unique up to $\scong$ and permutation. The primes among flat processes
are exactly the receives and the sends.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Consider the rewriting of Section~\ref{sec:calculus}: (CHAOS), (DIST) and
idempotence oriented left to right, and (FRAME) used to make binding
occurrences undecorated. It terminates, by a standard polynomial
interpretation for the distributive and absorbing rules. It is confluent
modulo AC; the critical pairs join, for instance
$\chaos\mid(Q+R)$ rewrites to $\chaos$ directly and through
$\chaos\mid Q+\chaos\mid R$, and $P\mid(Q+Q)$ rewrites to $P\mid Q$ through
either idempotence or (DIST). Hence $P\scong Q$ if and only if
$\nf(P)=_{AC}\nf(Q)$. In a flat process no rule applies at top level, so
$\nf(q_1\mid\cdots\mid q_k)=\nf(q_1)\mid\cdots\mid\nf(q_k)$ with each $q_i$ a
receive or a send, and AC-equality of two such terms is equality of the
multisets of their components.
\end{proof}

Behavioural unique decomposition, which is what the roots of~\cite{rft}
require, is a different and harder question; see
Section~\ref{sec:further}. For a flat $P$ write $M_P$ for its multiset of
primes. By Lemma~\ref{lem:auto} the action maps primes to
primes, so $H$ acts on primes and on such multisets, and each prime $q$ has a
stabiliser $\Stab_H(q)=\Stab(q)\cap H$.

\begin{theorem}[Twisted divisibility]\label{thm:twisted}
Let $H\le G$ be finite and $P$ flat. Then $P$ has an $H$-twisted root if and
only if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $M_P$ is $H$-invariant, and
\item for every prime $q$, $|\Stab_H(q)|$ divides the multiplicity $M_P(q)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
($\Rightarrow$) Let $\prod_H R\scong P$. Then $R$ is flat: a top-level sum
of distinct configurations in $\nf(R)$ would survive into $\nf(\prod_H R)$,
since parallel composition cancels in the free commutative monoid of
Lemma~\ref{lem:upd}, and a top-level $\chaos$ would make $P\scong\chaos$. So
by Lemma~\ref{lem:upd},
$M_P=\sum_{h\in H}\act{h}{M_R}$, which is invariant. For a prime $q$,
\[
M_P(q)=\sum_{h\in H}M_R(\act{h^{-1}}{q})
=|\Stab_H(q)|\sum_{q'\in H\cdot q}M_R(q'),
\]
because each $q'$ in the orbit of $q$ equals $\act{h^{-1}}{q}$ for exactly
$|\Stab_H(q)|$ elements $h$.
($\Leftarrow$) Choose one prime $q$ from each $H$-orbit in $M_P$ and let $R$
be the composition of the chosen primes, each with multiplicity
$M_P(q)/|\Stab_H(q)|$. By the same count, $\prod_H R$ gives each $q'$ in the
orbit of a chosen $q$ multiplicity
$|\Stab_H(q')|\cdot M_P(q)/|\Stab_H(q)|=M_P(q)=M_P(q')$, using that stabilisers
along an orbit are conjugate and that $M_P$ is invariant.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:roots}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $H$ fixes every prime of $P$, then $P$ has an $H$-twisted root if and
  only if $|H|$ divides every multiplicity. This is the root criterion
  of~\cite{rft} with $n=|H|$.
\item If $H$ acts freely on the primes of $P$, then $P$ has an $H$-twisted root
  if and only if $M_P$ is $H$-invariant: symmetry replaces divisibility.
\item By Theorem~\ref{thm:counting}, $|\Stab_H(q)|$ divides
  $\gcd(|H|,|\FN(q)|)$. A prime whose number of free names is coprime to $|H|$
  imposes no divisibility condition.
\item Chaos is its own twisted root for every $H$: $\prod_H\chaos\scong\chaos$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The stabiliser is the replication factor}

Theorem~\ref{thm:twisted} interpolates between two familiar ways of spreading
a system over $n$ sites. Take an orbit $O$ of primes with stabiliser $S$. In
$\prod_H R$, each prime of $O$ is contributed by exactly $|S|$ of the copies.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item When $S=H$ every copy contributes the same component: this is
  \emph{replication}, and the copies are those of an ordinary root.
\item When $S$ is trivial each component comes from exactly one copy, and the
  copies partition the system among themselves: this is \emph{sharding}, in
  which every site runs the same program in its own frame.
\end{itemize}
In general the replication factor of a component is the order of its
stabiliser, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:roots}(iii) it divides both $|H|$ and its
number of free names.

This bears directly on the arbitration problem of~\cite{rft}. There, copies of
a root contend for the same linear messages, and an arbiter must be installed
at every contention site, at a cost of $\Theta(n)$ rendezvous per site. In a
twisted root, identical primes, and hence identical channels, are shared only
among the $|S|$ copies that replicate them. The contention that requires
arbitration is confined to orbits with non-trivial stabiliser, and its width
is $|S|$ rather than $|H|$.

\paragraph{Coupling.}
Copies with trivial stabilisers can still interact, through channels they
share. Let
\[
C(R)=\{\,h\in H\setminus\{e\} : \code(m)\scong\act{h}{\code(n)}
\text{ for some } m,n\in\FN(R)\,\}.
\]
In the initial configuration, copies $\act{k}{R}$ and $\act{k'}{R}$ have a
redex between them only if $k^{-1}k'\in C(R)$, so the static coupling between
copies is the Cayley graph of $H$ with connection set $C(R)$. For the ring of
Section~\ref{sec:rings}, $C(R)=\{r,r^{-1}\}$, since philosopher $k$ shares fork
$f_{k+1}$ with philosopher $k+1$; $R$ is a twisted root of $\ring_n$ with trivial
stabilisers, and its coupling graph is the cycle. When $C(R)=\emptyset$
the copies are disjoint in the initial configuration. Whether they stay
disjoint depends on the names they can construct at run time, which is the
namespace question that rho always raises and that the action does not settle.

\paragraph{Rotations again.}
With $G=SO(3)$ and $H$ the icosahedral group, a twisted root spreads a system
over $60$ sites at the orientations of the icosahedron. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:roots}(iii), a component with a number of free names
coprime to $60$ is sharded, never replicated.

\section{Dynamic frames and error correction}\label{sec:ecc}

The applications so far use the action from outside the calculus: a
process is rotated, and we reason about what follows. Error correction asks
for the opposite. A process must measure a frame at run time and undo it.
Decorations as defined in Section~\ref{sec:calculus} are literal group
elements fixed in the program text, so a process cannot rotate what it has
received by an amount it has received. This section adds the one feature that
allows it, shows that it costs nothing in the metatheory proved so far, and
then uses it to build a decoder that corrects frame drift on every copy of a
message, together with one further fault, by triple redundancy. The cost of
the feature is not in the metatheory but in deciding equality, and
Section~\ref{sec:wordcost} is devoted to it.

\subsection{The extension}

Replace the data of $\calc$ by \emph{words} over literals and data variables:
\[
w \;::=\; g \;\mid\; \delta \;\mid\; w\cdot w \;\mid\; w^{-1}
\qquad (g\in G),
\]
and allow a word as the decoration of a process variable, $\fr{w}{X}$. The
old forms are special cases: a literal datum is the word $g$, and the
decorated data variable $\fr{g}{\delta}$ is the word $g\cdot\delta$. Two
restrictions keep the extension conservative.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Scope.} Every data variable occurring in a word is bound by an
  enclosing pattern.
\item \emph{Patterns are unchanged.} Within a pattern, decorations and data
  are of the old forms $g$, $\fr{g}{X}$ and $g\cdot\delta$, with $g$ a literal.
  Words that mention variables appear only in bodies.
\end{itemize}

The action multiplies words on the left, $\act{g}{w}=g\cdot w$ and
$\act{g}{\fr{w}{X}}=\fr{g\cdot w}{X}$. A substitution evaluates words:
$w\sigma$ is the element of $G$ obtained by replacing each $\delta$ by
$\sigma(\delta)$ and multiplying out, and $(\fr{w}{X})\sigma =
\act{(w\sigma)}{\sigma(X)}$. (FRAME) becomes the replacement of a bound data
variable $\delta$ by $h\cdot\delta$ throughout its scope, including in its
pattern, and of $\fr{w}{Z}$ by $\fr{w\cdot h}{Z}$ for a bound process
variable $Z$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dyn}
In the extended calculus:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item if $P$ is closed and $P\red P'$ then $P'$ is closed;
\item $\act{g}{(P\sigma)}=(\act{g}{P})\sigma$ and
  $\match(\act{g}{\pi},\act{g}{n})=\match(\pi,n)$;
\item Theorem~\ref{thm:equivariance}, Corollary~\ref{cor:labels} and
  Theorem~\ref{thm:counting} hold verbatim.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) By scope, every variable of a word in the continuation of a receive is
bound either by that receive, whose matcher instantiates it, or by an
enclosing receive, which has already fired if the term is closed. (ii) At a
decorated variable,
$(\act{g}{\fr{w}{X}})\sigma=\act{(g\cdot w\sigma)}{\sigma(X)}
=\act{g}{(\fr{w}{X})\sigma}$; patterns are unchanged, so the matching clause
is Lemma~\ref{lem:subst}. (iii) The proofs use only (ii), the automorphism
properties of Lemma~\ref{lem:auto}, which hold because left multiplication of
words commutes with (FRAME)'s right multiplication, and freeness on atoms. For
a variable atom the frame is now a word, $\phi(\fr{w}{X})=w$, and
$g\cdot w=w$ forces $g=e$ in the free product of $G$ with the free group on
the variables.
\end{proof}

Nothing unbound is ever sent. A message in a body, such as
$\out{y}{\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X}}{e}$, exists only once its guard has fired, and
by then the substitution has discharged the decoration.

\subsection{Frame-fixing}

The basic gadget re-expresses whatever arrives on $x$ in the identity frame
and forwards it to $y$:
\[
N_{x,y} \;=\; \rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{x}{\out{y}{\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X}}{e}}.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Frame-fixing]\label{prop:framefix}
For all closed $Q$ and all $g\in G$,
\[
N_{x,y}\mid\out{x}{\act{g}{Q}}{g\,d}\;\red\;\out{y}{\act{d^{-1}}{Q}}{e}.
\]
The output does not depend on $g$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The subjects are equal, so both coefficients are $e$ and the reduct is a
single term by Proposition~\ref{prop:conservative}. The matcher is
$\{\act{g}{Q}/X,\ g\,d/\delta\}$, and
$(\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X})\sigma=\act{(d^{-1}g^{-1})}{\act{g}{Q}}=\act{d^{-1}}{Q}$.
\end{proof}

A message carries its own frame. If payload and datum are rotated together,
by drift along a channel or by a sender working in the wrong coordinates,
the datum records the rotation and $N$ undoes it. Such errors need no
redundancy at all. A message sent as $\out{x}{Q}{e}$ and drifted by any $g$
is delivered by $N_{x,y}$ as exactly $\out{y}{Q}{e}$.

What frame-fixing cannot repair is \emph{skew}: an error that rotates the
payload and the datum by different amounts. If $\out{x}{Q}{e}$ arrives as
$\out{x}{\act{g}{Q}}{h}$, then $N_{x,y}$ delivers $\act{s}{Q}$ with skew
$s=h^{-1}g$. By freeness, $\act{s}{Q}\scong Q$ exactly when $s\in\Stab(Q)$.
The symmetries of the payload are therefore precisely the skews that are
both undetectable and harmless. Every other skew changes the term, and
detecting it needs a second opinion.

\subsection{A worked example: drift-tolerant triple redundancy}

Fix closed names $c_1,c_2,c_3$, $t_1,t_2,t_3$, $\ell$ and $y$, all with datum
$e$ and pairwise non-congruent codes. Indices are taken mod $3$. Put
\[
\langle\!\langle j,P\rangle\!\rangle \;=\; \qn{\out{t_j}{P}{e}}{e},
\]
a name whose code is the payload $P$ tagged with $j$; two such names are
equal exactly when their tags agree and their payloads are congruent.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
To transmit $Q$ on $y$, send three copies:
$\mathit{Enc}(Q)=\out{c_1}{Q}{e}\mid\out{c_2}{Q}{e}\mid\out{c_3}{Q}{e}$.

\paragraph{Fault model.}
Each copy $\out{c_i}{Q}{e}$ may arrive as $\out{c_i}{\act{g_i}{Q}}{h_i}$ for
arbitrary $g_i,h_i\in G$, or be \emph{erased}, that is, not arrive. Write
$s_i=h_i^{-1}g_i$ for its skew. Copy $i$ is \emph{faulty} if it is erased or
$s_i\notin\Stab(Q)$. Drift, the case $g_i=h_i$, is not a fault, and may
affect every copy.

\paragraph{Decoder.}
Copy $i$ is fixed into the identity frame, announces the result under tag
$i$, and listens for its predecessor's announcement under tag $i-1$, on a
name it builds from its own fixed content:
\begin{align*}
D_i &= \rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{c_i}{\bigl(A_i\mid L_i\bigr)},\\
A_i &= \out{\langle\!\langle i,\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X}\rangle\!\rangle}
           {\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X}}{e},\\
L_i &= \rcv{\qn{Z}{\varepsilon}}
           {\langle\!\langle i-1,\fr{\delta^{-1}}{X}\rangle\!\rangle}
           {\rcv{\qn{W}{\omega}}{\ell}{\out{y}{Z}{e}}},\\
\mathit{Dec} &= D_1\mid D_2\mid D_3\mid\out{\ell}{\nil}{e}.
\end{align*}
The announcement of copy $i-1$ meets the listener of copy $i$ exactly when
their fixed contents are congruent: the gate compares the two codes. The
message on $\ell$ is a latch that lets one agreeing pair deliver.

\begin{theorem}[Drift-tolerant triple redundancy]\label{thm:tmr}
Let at most one copy be faulty. Then every maximal reduction sequence of
$\mathit{Enc}'(Q)\mid\mathit{Dec}$, where $\mathit{Enc}'(Q)$ is the encoder
after the faults and drift, terminates in a state containing exactly one
message on $y$, and its payload is congruent to $Q$. No reachable state
contains a message on $y$ with any other payload.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every subject in the system is one of the fixed names, an
$\langle\!\langle j,P\rangle\!\rangle$, or $\ell$, all with datum $e$, so by
Proposition~\ref{prop:conservative} every communication is ordinary and no
sums arise. Each receive fires at most once and nothing is replicated, so
every sequence terminates.

\emph{Fixing.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:framefix}, each copy that arrives
yields fixed content $q_i=\act{s_i}{Q}$, and $q_i\scong Q$ if and only if
copy $i$ is not faulty.

\emph{Tests.} The only listener on a name with tag $i$ is $L_{i+1}$, so the
announcement $A_i$ can be consumed only by $L_{i+1}$, and it is consumed
exactly when $q_i\scong q_{i+1}$. Call this the test $(i\to i+1)$. A test that
fires involves two copies that arrived, at most one of which is faulty, so
both have content congruent to $Q$, and the payload $Z$ it binds is
$q_i\scong Q$. Hence no message on $y$ ever carries anything else.

\emph{Delivery.} Let $b$ be the faulty copy, or any copy if none is faulty.
The copies $b+1$ and $b+2$ both arrive with content congruent to $Q$, so in
any maximal sequence the test $(b+1\to b+2)$ fires, because its announcement
has no other consumer. Its continuation then waits on $\ell$. The single
message on $\ell$ is consumed by exactly one waiting continuation, so exactly
one message is sent on $y$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Reading the result.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Drift is free.} Ordinary triple redundancy compares raw copies,
  so drift on every copy defeats it. Here each copy is fixed before
  comparison, so arbitrary drift on all three copies is tolerated in
  addition to the one fault.
\item \emph{Erasures and skews are the same fault.} An erased copy never
  announces or listens; a skewed copy fails both of its tests. Either way the
  test between the other two goes through.
\item \emph{The syndrome is the residue.} The final state retains the
  announcements and listeners of the tests that did not fire. A faulty copy
  $b$ leaves both $(b-1\to b)$ and $(b\to b+1)$ parked; a fault-free run
  leaves two listeners parked on $\ell$. Reading the residue identifies the
  faulty copy.
\item \emph{The comparator is communication.} No equality test is added to
  the calculus. The gate of (COMM) compares codes, and building a channel
  name from content turns that comparison into a rendezvous.
\item \emph{Benign skews are symmetries.} Skews in $\Stab(Q)$ are not faults.
  By Theorem~\ref{thm:counting}, when $\FA(Q)$ is finite and non-empty this set
  is a finite group of order dividing $|\FA(Q)|$, so for any infinite $G$
  almost every skew is detected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The cost of the word problem}\label{sec:wordcost}

The extension is free in the metatheory and not free in practice. Every
communication evaluates the gate $\code(x)\scong\code(u)$, and every match
against a literal compares data, so the calculus is effective only if
equality of codes is decidable. With words, that equality acquires a
group-theoretic core in two places.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Closed words: arithmetic in $G$.} A substitution multiplies out a
  closed word. Reduction therefore needs $G$ to be a \emph{computable} group:
  elements with exact representations, and computable multiplication,
  inversion and equality. For finite $G$ this is table lookup. For
  $\mathbb{Z}^n$ it is integer arithmetic. For a continuous group such as
  $SO(3)$ it is not directly available. Floating-point rotations make the gate
  unsound, since two codes that should be equal may not compare equal. One must
  instead work in a countable subgroup with exact representations, such as
  rotations given by quaternions with rational or algebraic coefficients, and
  then the size of the representation grows with the length of the words
  multiplied.
\item \emph{Open words: the word problem.} Codes are processes, and processes
  contain binders. Comparing two codes up to $\scong$ descends under receives,
  where words still mention bound variables: deciding
  $\rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{x}{\out{y}{\fr{\delta^{-1}\delta}{X}}{e}}\scong
  \rcv{\qn{X}{\delta}}{x}{\out{y}{X}{e}}$ requires knowing that
  $\delta^{-1}\delta=e$ for every value of $\delta$. In general, equality of
  open words is the word problem for the free product $G*F(V)$ of $G$ with the
  free group on the bound data variables. That problem is decidable exactly
  when the word problem for $G$ is, and its cost is essentially the cost for
  $G$ together with free reduction.
\end{enumerate}

The second cost arises at run time, not only in the analysis. The decoder of
Theorem~\ref{thm:tmr} compares fixed contents $q_i$, and when $Q$ itself
contains receives with words in their bodies, the comparison is an instance of
the word problem. The same holds for stabiliser computation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:computable}), for orbit canonicalisation in
Section~\ref{sec:reduction}, and for the normal forms behind
Lemma~\ref{lem:upd}: each congruence check now carries a word problem.

How much that costs depends entirely on $G$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
group & word problem\\
\midrule
finite, e.g.\ $\mathbb{Z}/n$ or a finite rotation group & constant time per
  letter\\
finitely generated abelian & linear\\
free & linear, by free reduction\\
hyperbolic & linear, by Dehn's algorithm\\
automatic & quadratic~\cite{epstein}\\
finitely presented, in general & undecidable~\cite{novikov,boone}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For the groups used in the applications of this note, cyclic and finite
rotation groups, the cost is negligible. But the extension is safe to adopt
only for a group with an effective, ideally linear, word problem. For a
finitely presented group with an unsolvable word problem, the gate is
undecidable and the reduction relation is not effective.

\paragraph{Why patterns stay as they are.}
The restriction that patterns contain no words is what keeps matching at the
level of the word problem. A pattern such as $\qn{\fr{\delta}{X}}{\delta}$
would make matching a matter of solving equations over $G$, not merely
evaluating words. That is a much harder problem: decidable for free groups
only through Makanin's algorithm~\cite{makanin}, at very high cost, and
undecidable in general. Keeping dynamic decorations in bodies means matching
still only inverts literals.

\paragraph{An intensional alternative.}
If the word problem for $G$ is expensive, open words can be compared
syntactically after free reduction, evaluating only closed words. Equality is
then decidable for any computable $G$, at the price of a finer congruence:
codes that differ only by a group identity, such as $\fr{\delta^{-1}\delta}{X}$
against $X$, become different channels. The finer equality only ever
separates codes that the intended one identifies, so it is safe where
equality licenses an action. The decoder of Theorem~\ref{thm:tmr} never
delivers a wrong payload under it, and still delivers on fault-free and
drifted copies, whose fixed contents are $Q$ itself; at worst a non-identity
benign skew is treated as a fault. Symmetry reduction misses some symmetries
but never invents one. It is not the intended congruence, and choosing it
should be stated as a choice.

\section{Further work}\label{sec:further}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Behavioural twisted roots.} The structural theorem above should be
  lifted to $\prod_H R\approx P$ for an equivariant behavioural equivalence,
  relative to an observer as in~\cite{rft}, with orbit barbs as observations.
\item \emph{Arbitration in twisted roots.} The claim that arbitration is
  confined to orbits with non-trivial stabiliser should be made precise by
  combining the arbiter of~\cite{rft} with the coupling graph.
\item \emph{Lehmann--Rabin in $\calc$.} State and prove the impossibility of
  deterministic symmetric solutions with $\langle r\rangle$-invariance as the
  notion of symmetry, and identify the symmetry breaker that a randomised
  solution needs. A probabilistic reading of summation is one candidate.
\item \emph{Transport on isotropic channels.} The behaviour of isotropic
  rings under the coded gate and under $K$-gates, where communication moves
  agents along a Cayley graph.
\item \emph{Symmetric decoders and longer codes.} Write the decoder of
  Theorem~\ref{thm:tmr} itself as an orbit product, with the comparison done
  in rotated frames, and generalise from three copies to repetition codes on
  orbits with stabiliser $S$, correcting $\lfloor(|S|-1)/2\rfloor$ faults.
\item \emph{Symmetry reduction in practice.} Implement stabiliser computation
  and orbit canonicalisation in the spatial logic model checker, and measure
  the reduction on rings and twisted roots.
\item \emph{Behavioural unique decomposition.} Lemma~\ref{lem:upd} is
  structural. Up to bisimilarity, unique decomposition fails for the full
  rho calculus, since replication obtained by reflection is idempotent. The
  argument of Milner and Moller~\cite{milner-moller}, in the generality of
  decomposition orders~\cite{luttik-vo}, needs a measure that adds across
  parallel composition and transitions that decompose. The phase-one
  translation into the rho combinators~\cite{rhocomb3} supplies both: it is
  homomorphic on parallel composition and fully abstract relative to encoded
  contexts, and its weighted atom count adds across components. The
  candidate theorem is unique decomposition up to context-labelled
  bisimilarity for the fragment without drop in process position, proved in
  the target and transferred back, with divisor-closure of the image as the
  algebraic hinge. Corollary~\ref{cor:labels} then makes behavioural twisted
  roots statable.
\end{itemize}
